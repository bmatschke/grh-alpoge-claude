# Why should a chain of theta lifts say anything about primes?

The goal is a square-root upper bound for fluctuations in a weighted count of primes. Alpöge’s idea is to build an arithmetic object whose **forced divisibility carries the weights of the prime count**. At a prime of the relevant splitting type, one obtains a lower bound on divisibility in certain derivatives of theta functions. Repeating the construction accumulates these gains. The same object lives in a complex space of automorphic forms, where its size and the relative positions of its images can be estimated. A global inequality for lattices limits how much divisibility those sizes can support. That limit becomes the upper bound on primes. [Alpöge’s account, pp. 1, 3–4](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) · [The two sides of the comparison, CMP.8–10](conditional_main_v42/conditional_main.tex)

## The ancestor is Stepanov’s method

On a smooth projective curve, a nonzero rational function has equally many zeros and poles, counted with multiplicity. If one constructs a function vanishing to order at least $m$ at each of $N$ chosen points, then $mN$ is at most the degree of its pole divisor. The ingenuity is to force the zeros, keep the pole degree small and ensure the function survives. Stepanov used this idea to count points over finite fields. Alpöge explicitly names it as the ancestor of his argument. In the number-field construction the forced zeros become finite-prime divisibility, a bound on poles becomes a bound on arithmetic degree, and the chain of theta lifts plays the role of multiplicity. [Stepanov, original hyperelliptic case](https://doi.org/10.1070/IM1969v003n05ABEH000834) · [Alpöge, p. 1](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf#page=1)

## Where the prime weight comes from

Take one imaginary quadratic field $K$, with character $\chi_K$, and omit the primes dividing its discriminant. Consider two positive jet orders $a,w$. In the one-step model the gain from an inert prime $p$ contains terms $(a/p^j-w)_+$ for odd $j$, where $z_+=\max(z,0)$. The coefficient characteristic of the height-two local calculation is

$$\frac{p}{p^2-1}=\frac1p+\frac1{p^3}+\frac1{p^5}+\cdots.$$

This particular number has a classical precedent. Katz’s Main Theorem 3.11(I) gives divisibility by $p^{\lfloor np/(p^2-1)\rfloor}$ for the value at the origin of the $n$th invariant derivative of an integral function in his trace-zero space on a height-two Lubin–Tate formal group. Here trace zero means that the sum of the translates by the $p$-torsion subgroup vanishes. The theta construction has several variables, integral sections, and prescribed differential operators. Passing from Katz’s setting to those objects requires a separate local argument. [Katz, pp. 669, 672](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~nmk/old/divcongCartier.pdf#page=6) · [The actual determinant derivatives](determinant_jet_v57/main.tex)

The connection to primes is already exact at the level of the weights. Set $x=a/w$. At an unramified prime $1-\chi_K(p^j)$ equals two precisely when $p$ is inert and $j$ is odd. Since $\Lambda(p^j)=\log p$, we get

$$
2\sum_{p\ {\rm inert}}\log p\sum_{j\ {\rm odd},\ j\ge1}(a/p^j-w)_+
=
w\sum_{\substack{p\nmid {\rm disc}(K)\\j\ge1}}
(1-\chi_K(p^j))\,\Lambda(p^j)\,(x/p^j-1)_+ .
$$

The left side displays the local divisibility profile. The right side is a smoothed prime sum with a quadratic twist. In the full proof the second weight depends on the place and on the lift, ramification has its own contributions, and divisibility gives an inequality with errors. Orthogonality of characters of larger abelian groups and averaging over translates extract the combination for the desired Dirichlet character. This one calculation explains the choice of both the smoothing kernel and the auxiliary fields. [Local gain, GLG.4](global_local_gain_v46/main.tex) · [Exact prime-power expansion and character averaging, CMP1–2](conditional_main_v42/conditional_main.tex) · [Huang’s one-correspondence model, §5.2](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00833v1)

## Why the construction has two descriptions

For a nonzero integer $n$, the identity $\sum_p v_p(n)\log p=\log|n|$ compares finite-prime divisibility with ordinary size. For an integral square matrix of full rank, apply it to the determinant. For lattices over a number field, finite indices and archimedean determinant norms enter the arithmetic degree. The degree divided by the rank is the arithmetic slope. Bost’s slope inequality for an injective map $E\to F$ bounds the mean slope of $E$ by the largest slope of $F$ plus the height of the map. [Bost, Proposition 4.5, equation (4.11)](https://numdam.org/item/10.1007/s10240-001-8191-3.pdf#page=39)

Here there are many period maps $B_u:N_u\to C$ into one target, indexed by the choices made in the chain. In the arithmetic description their values come from integral theta data. It is in this description that one can count divisibility. In the analytic description they are period integrals of automorphic forms with the Petersson inner product. It is there that a relative trace formula can estimate sizes and pairwise overlaps. If every map landed in the same line in a large target, an estimate for the largest target slope would say little about the mean slope of the target. The proof needs the collective images to occupy most directions even after a controlled loss of source directions. This is why obtaining mixed inner products and identifying the two descriptions both matter. The arithmetic heights of the individual maps must also be bounded in their own metrics. [Gram bounds and the mean-slope conclusion](gram_frame/gram_frame_section.tex) · [Use of the original period maps, GFA7–8](global_assembly/main.tex)

Chenxin Huang gives a particularly helpful geometric reading. For a chain of length two, think of evaluating forms on a set of CM points. Quantitative sampling asks how evenly these evaluations detect a space of forms. Volume estimates concern the slopes of the lattices of forms and values. For longer chains, the cusp forms involved generally do not give holomorphic sections of a single line bundle on that Shimura variety. The evaluation is built through successive theta lifts. Its agreement with the Bessel period of the descended form is a substantive comparison, labeled Hypothesis 3.6.1 in Huang’s exposition. Huang compares this architecture with Calegari–Dimitrov–Tang’s use of slopes in arithmetic holonomy. In the arithmetic holonomy application, a prime estimate enters a rank bound in Diophantine approximation. In the theta construction, geometric control of the evaluations yields a prime estimate. [Huang, §§1.3–1.4, 3.3–3.7 and 6](https://hexagonmath.org/2611.00833v1) · [Calegari–Dimitrov–Tang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04156)

## Why square-root length, and how the critical line enters

Each lift adds a divisibility gain. Increasing the number $m$ of lifts spreads the initial cost over more steps, but the later steps incur costs of their own. In the reconstruction's conditional main estimate, two competing terms in the normalized error are bounded by $2x/m$ and $2mx^\varepsilon/d_1$, where $d_1$ is a density parameter controlled in the chosen family. At the level of powers of $x$, the balance is between $x/m$ and $m$. A depth of order $x^{1/2+O(\varepsilon)}$ puts both at square-root scale, provided the density loss and the other errors meet their bounds. The hard work is quantitative enough control to make this balance valid. Alpöge’s account records an earlier cost calculation which appeared to stop at exponent $2/3$, followed by the use of a common integral structure across characters and further averaging. [Main inequality and balance of errors, CMP.7–14](conditional_main_v42/conditional_main.tex) · [Alpöge, items 12–16](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf#page=4)

For zeta, the quantity to bound is the centered sum

$$G(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n)(x/n-1)-x\log x+(1+\gamma)x.$$

An upper bound $G(x)\ll_\eta x^{1/2+\eta}$ on arbitrarily late entire windows $[Y,Y^{3/2}]$, for every small $\eta>0$, suffices. A zero to the right of the critical line produces a pole of the Mellin transform. The window argument localizes high derivatives of that transform to a window and shows that such a pole precludes the required upper bound. For a primitive Dirichlet target the proof uses a fixed positive combination of centered sums for the primitive characters inducing its powers. The multiplicities of a shared zero add. The functional equation then deals with the other half of the strip. [The analytic deduction, AQ2–4](analytic_envelope/analytic_envelope.tex) · [Original centered sums and window condition](https://github.com/CocoSgt/riemann-hypothesis-lean/blob/7cbf7a0c32fca1badf05931879a37c3f8a2b03b4/src/Development/AutomorphicArithmetic/DedekindLFunctions/CharFamily.lean#L10-L45)

For the current Bessel problem there is a choice of mathematical target. An identity for the actual normalized periods proves the original comparison. For the mean-slope argument, identifying their **image subspaces** under one common linear isomorphism already suffices when the arithmetic heights are available separately. A direct quantitative spanning estimate on the arithmetic maps is a further route to that same slope conclusion. The companion note proves these implications and identifies the largest possible loss for each fixed family with a known invariant, the discrepancy of a quiver representation. Published noncommutative rank theorems give an exact way to certify its value. Its two-line example shows why the relative positions of the images matter.
